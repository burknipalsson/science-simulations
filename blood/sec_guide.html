Field guide

What's in a drop

A microlitre of blood, a cube one millimetre on a side, holds about five million red cells, five to ten thousand white cells and a few hundred thousand platelets, all carried by plasma. These are the players in the simulation above and the numbers it is built on.

7.8 µm across · 4.2–6.2 million per µL

The red cell: a bag with spare skin

A red cell holds 94 µm³ of haemoglobin solution inside 135 µm² of membrane. A sphere of that volume would need only 100 µm², so the cell has a third more skin than it needs. That slack is why it can fold into almost any shape without stretching: its membrane resists change of area strongly but shearing hardly at all.

5–7 µm wide · 0.5–1 mm long

The capillary

Capillaries are narrower than the cells they carry. Cells pass in single file, folded into parachutes (Skalak & Brånemark filmed them in 1969: a biconcave disc with its upstream face pushed in), at 0.2–1.5 mm/s. A red cell can get through a tube as narrow as about 2.7 µm.

plasma 1.1–1.3 mPa·s · inside a cell about 5.6

The cell-free layer

In any vessel wider than a capillary, the cells drift away from the wall, leaving a sleeve of plain plasma 1–2 µm thick. The cell-rich core slides on it. This is why blood in small vessels is thinner, and carries fewer cells, than blood in big ones.

shear modulus 5–10 µN/m

How soft is a red cell?

Pulled between two glass beads with laser tweezers, a cell stretches from 7.8 to about 16 µm under 0.2 nanonewtons (Mills et al. 2004). Stiffness is health: one study found stored blood stiffening about fivefold in three weeks in the bag. Make the cells stiffer here and the soft parachute becomes a bullet that needs more pressure to push.

irreversibly sickled cells: 20 µN/m vs 7 healthy

Sickle cell disease

Haemoglobin S polymerises when it loses oxygen, but only after a delay that shortens roughly as the 30th power of its concentration (Hofrichter, Ross & Eaton 1974). Delays span the capillary transit time, so many cells escape before they stiffen; those that don't, and the cells left permanently sickled, are what block vessels.

P. falciparum: up to 10× stiffer

Malaria and the spleen

As the parasite grows, its host cell stiffens: about 18 µN/m at the ring stage, 36 at the trophozoite, against 7 for a healthy cell (Mills et al. 2007), and the ring stage stiffens further to 30 at a fever of 41 °C. The spleen strains blood through narrow slits, and holds back the cells that won't bend.

4,500–11,000 white cells · 150,000–400,000 platelets per µL

The rest of the traffic

A neutrophil is a sphere of about 300 µm³, some 8 µm across, that crawls at around 18 µm a minute; in David Rogers' 1950s film one chases a staphylococcus, a bacterium 1 µm across, through a blood smear. Platelets are discs 2–3 µm wide. Both get pushed to the wall by the red cells, which is where they are needed. They are not simulated here.
