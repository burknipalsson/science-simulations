Predicting eclipses

Where the Moon's shadow falls

An eclipse needs the Sun, Moon and Earth to line up to within a fraction of a degree, so it is a sharp test of a simulation. This one finds eclipses by itself: it looks at every new and full moon, traces the Moon's shadow onto the spinning, slightly flattened Earth, and times how long totality lasts. Pick an eclipse to watch its shadow cross the globe, or step through the eclipses one by one.

      Mercury and Einstein

      The missing 43 arcseconds

      Mercury's oval orbit slowly turns, so the point where it passes closest to the Sun creeps around. In 1859 Urbain Le Verrier found that the other planets' pull explains almost all of the turning, but not quite: 43 arcseconds per century were left over. Some astronomers blamed an unseen planet, "Vulcan". In 1915 Einstein's general relativity produced exactly the missing amount, the first evidence that his theory was right.

      To make the effect visible, this small model keeps Mercury and the Sun only and slows light down. Relativity's pull grows with (v/c)², so light 100 times slower turns the orbit 10,000 times faster, and the ellipse traces a rosette. With Newton alone the ellipse would close on itself forever.

      The asteroid belt

      Gaps carved by Jupiter

      Sort the real asteroids by their distance from the Sun and gaps appear, first noticed by Daniel Kirkwood in 1866. They sit where an asteroid would circle the Sun exactly 3, 5/2, 7/3 or 2 times for each of Jupiter's orbits. There Jupiter tugs on it at the same point again and again, stretching its orbit until it crosses Mars or Earth and is thrown out. Where the timing is 3:2 or 1:1 the same regularity protects instead: those are the Hildas and the Trojans.

      Field guide

      Things to watch for

      Each note below sets up the simulation to show you. Planets are drawn larger than life so you can see them. Switch on true sizes to see how empty space really is.

      JPL DE441 · 1 January 2026

      Where everything is today

      The starting point is JPL's own ephemeris, fitted to decades of radar, laser and spacecraft tracking. From there, gravity alone moves every body: 15 massive bodies pull on each other, and the asteroids feel the planets. Run the clock back 2,000 years and the planets still land within about 2 arcseconds of JPL's positions.

      299,792 km per second

      Sunlight is already old

      Light from the Sun takes 8 minutes 20 seconds to reach Earth, 43 minutes to reach Jupiter and more than 4 hours to reach Neptune. Voyager 1's radio signals now take almost a day. Flash the Sun and follow the light outward at true speed.

      barycentre · up to 2.2 solar radii

      The Sun wobbles

      Planets don't orbit the Sun's centre but the centre of mass of the whole system. Jupiter alone drags that point about one solar radius from the Sun's middle, and with Saturn the Sun loops around it every few decades. Astronomers find planets around other stars by catching this same wobble.

      +43″ per century

      Mercury's turning orbit

      Mercury's orbit turns about 575 arcseconds a century. Newton's gravity explains 532 of them; Einstein's curved spacetime supplies the rest. This simulation carries Einstein's correction for every planet, and measures the effect itself.

      2 August 2027 · 6 min 23 s

      The eclipse of the century

      Next August the Moon's shadow crosses southern Spain, North Africa and Egypt. Near Luxor, day turns to night for 6 minutes 23 seconds, the longest total eclipse seen from land between 1991 and 2114. The simulation finds it from its own orbits.

      1977 – 1989 · four planets

      Voyager's slingshots

      Voyager 2 left Earth too slowly to escape the Sun. Swinging past Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, it stole a little of each planet's orbital motion and sped up by about 10 km/s at Jupiter alone. The planets lined up for this only once every 175 years.

      L4 · L5 · the 3 : 2 resonance

      Jupiter's Trojans and the Hilda triangle

      Turn the view with Jupiter and two swarms of Trojan asteroids hang still, 60° ahead of and behind the planet. The Hildas, circling three times for every two Jupiter orbits, trace a slowly breathing triangle between them.

      13 April 2029 · 32,000 km up

      Apophis passes close

      On Friday 13 April 2029 the 340-metre asteroid Apophis will pass closer than the satellites that carry TV signals, visible to the naked eye over Europe and Africa. Radar tracking has ruled out an impact for at least a century.

      perihelion 28 July 2061

      Halley comes back

      Halley's comet is heading back toward the Sun now, having turned around beyond Neptune in December 2023. It returns every 75 or 76 years, and its 2061 visit should be a much better show than the dim one of 1986.

      Checking the model

      The simulation against the real sky

      A simulation earns trust by matching measurements it wasn't built from. This one starts from JPL's positions for a single moment, 1 January 2026, then runs on physics alone. The table compares its results with JPL's DE441 ephemeris and NASA's eclipse catalogue for dates far from that start. The numbers are computed live in your browser, so they fill in as the background integration covers each date.

      The orbits use Einstein's equations for the Sun, planets and Moon (the same "EIH" equations JPL uses), Earth's equatorial bulge acting on the Moon, and the tidal push that makes the Moon recede by 3.8 cm a year. The Moon's starting motion was fitted by least squares to JPL's lunar orbit over 1876–2176, much as JPL fits its own orbit to laser ranging.

      A planet 2 arcseconds out is the width of a coin seen from 2 km away. A Moon error of 30″ shifts an eclipse by about a minute.

      BodyDistance from SunYearDayNotes

      How it works: fifteen bodies (the Sun, eight planets, the Moon, Pluto and the four largest asteroids) are integrated together in the solar system's centre-of-mass frame with a sixth-order symplectic method (Yoshida 1990) in half-day steps. Forces are Newtonian gravity plus the Einstein–Infeld–Hoffmann relativistic terms for the Sun, Earth, Moon and Jupiter, Earth's J₂ oblateness acting on the Moon, and a lunar tidal acceleration matching the measured 3.83 cm/yr recession. Masses and constants are JPL DE440's. Starting positions and velocities are JPL DE441's for 1 January 2026 (TDB); the Moon's were refined by least squares against DE441 over 1876–2176. The run is stored every 32 days and filled in both directions in the background.

      Asteroids are JPL small-body-database osculating elements for 9 June 2026 (all near-Earth objects, Trojans, Hildas, centaurs and trans-Neptunian objects in the catalogue, plus a random 22% of the numbered main belt). They move as massless particles on the graphics card: an exact Kepler orbit around the Sun, kicked every 4 days by Venus, Earth–Moon, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. They are accurate to hundredths of a degree for decades, but their paths are chaotic over centuries, so far from today their positions are typical rather than exact. Close encounters (such as Apophis in 2029) are not resolved in the swarm; Apophis's flyby and the spacecraft paths are JPL's trajectories. Comets and dwarf planets are integrated the same way without the jets of gas that nudge real comets.

      Eclipses: at each new and full moon the Moon's shadow axis is traced onto Earth using IAU 2006 precession, the main nutation terms and ΔT (the drift of Earth's rotation; Espenak–Meeus, joined to measured values). Light-time from the Sun and the Moon is included. ΔT before about 1600 is uncertain by minutes to hours, which moves ancient eclipse paths east or west.

      Data: JPL Horizons (DE441), JPL Small-Body Database, JPL DE440 constants. Textures: NASA Blue Marble, NASA Black Marble (Earth at night), NASA LRO (Moon). Everything is computed in the page; nothing is fetched while it runs.
