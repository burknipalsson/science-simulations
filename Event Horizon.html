How light falls

Parallel rays arrive from the left. Each one misses the center by a distance b, the impact parameter. Far out, rays barely bend. Below b = 3√3⁄2 rₛ ≈ 2.598 rₛ no ray escapes, and the ones just above that line wind around the hole before leaving. The glowing dots are a single wavefront moving together, so you can also watch light get delayed as it passes the hole.

The weak-field formula θ ≈ 2rₛ/b is what Eddington tested on starlight grazing the Sun in 1919. Near the hole it fails badly. Slide b toward 2.598 and the true bend goes to infinity.

Field guide

What you are looking at

Distances are in Schwarzschild radii, rₛ = 2GM/c², the radius of the event horizon. For the Sun that is 2.95 km. Each note below can set the simulator up to show you.

r = 1 rₛ  ·  b < 2.6 rₛ

The shadow is bigger than the hole

The horizon has radius 1 rₛ, but gravity pulls in every ray aimed within 2.6 rₛ of the center. So the dark disk you see is about 5.2 rₛ across, 2.6 times the size of the horizon. Turn on the sky grid and watch the lines crowd against its edge.

r = 1.5 rₛ

Light can orbit

At the photon sphere light can circle the hole, but the orbit is unstable. Rays that nearly hit it loop around once, twice, or more, and each extra half-loop makes a copy of the disk about eπ ≈ 23× thinner. Together these copies make the thin bright ring at the shadow's edge.

b ≈ 3–6 rₛ

You can see the back of the disk

Light from the far side of the disk bends up and over the hole. The disk seems to wrap above and below the shadow, the look made famous by Interstellar (2014). Switch off light bending to see how plain it would look without gravity.

v ≈ 0.5 c at the inner edge

One side is much brighter

Gas at the inner edge orbits at about half the speed of light. On the side moving toward you, light is beamed: brightness scales roughly as the fourth power of the Doppler factor, so it looks bluer and many times brighter. The side moving away turns dim and red.

√(1 − rₛ/r)

Clocks run slow down there

Hovering at 1.5 rₛ, your clock ticks at 0.58× the rate of a distant one, and light climbing out of the well loses energy on the way. Fly in and watch the readout. The whole sky ahead of you turns bright and blue, while the disk's glow turns red and dim.

r = 3 rₛ

The disk has an inner edge

Inside 3 rₛ, the innermost stable circular orbit, no orbit is stable. Gas that drifts past it plunges in within about one turn. That is why the disk has a sharp hole in the middle. Its hottest ring sits just outside, at about 4.1 rₛ.

Same shape, any size

Scale is only mass

A non-spinning black hole has one free parameter. Everything you see above scales with mass. A black hole ten times heavier looks the same, only ten times larger and ten times slower.

How it works: each pixel's ray is stepped through the Schwarzschild photon equation d²x/dλ² = −(3/2) h² x / r⁵ (units where rₛ = 1). This is exact for the non-spinning case. The disk is geometrically thin with a Novikov–Thorne-style temperature profile, T ∝ r−3/4(1 − √(3/r))1/4. Observed color and brightness use the combined Doppler and gravitational shift g, with intensity ∝ g⁴.

Simplifications: the hole does not spin (real ones do, which squashes and shifts the shadow), the disk emits blackbody light and is optically thin in places for looks, and brightness is tone-mapped for a screen.
