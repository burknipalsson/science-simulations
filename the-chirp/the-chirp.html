Two black holes circle each other, faster and closer, until they merge. The collision shakes space itself, and the ripples reached Earth 1.3 billion years later, stretching LIGO's 4 km arms by less than a proton's width. They also fall in the range of human hearing. Field guide

What you are hearing

Each note below can set up the simulation to show you. The sheet stands for space, seen from above the orbit. The dents are the black holes' pull, and the spiral ripples are the gravitational waves, moving outward at the speed of light, drawn true to scale against the orbit but with their height exaggerated enormously.

chirp mass ≈ 30 suns

The chirp gives away the masses

As the pair loses energy the orbit shrinks, speeds up, and the pitch rises. How fast the pitch climbs at a given pitch depends almost only on one blend of the two masses, the chirp mass. GW150914's came out near 30 suns. Heavier pairs chirp lower and end sooner. Neutron stars sweep up to over a thousand hertz.

Hanford ↔ Livingston · 3,002 km

Why the detectors were 7 ms apart

The waves travel at the speed of light, which takes 10 ms to cross the distance between the two LIGO sites. GW150914 reached Livingston first and Hanford 6.9 ms later, which put the source somewhere on a ring in the southern sky. Hanford's arms point differently, so its trace comes out flipped.

4 km arm · 4 × 10⁻¹⁸ m

A fraction of a proton

A strain of 10⁻²¹ changes a 4 km arm by about 4 × 10⁻¹⁸ m. At GW150914's loudest moment that was 1/400 of a proton's width, and for most of the chirp around 1/1000. LIGO bounces laser light hundreds of times along each arm and compares the two. The inset at the top right exaggerates the stretch about 10²⁰ times.

3 suns · 5 × 10⁴⁷ joules

Where the energy went

Three suns' worth of mass left as gravitational waves in a fraction of a second. At the peak the power was 3.6 × 10⁴⁹ watts, which LIGO put at more than 50 times the light of every star in the observable universe. The waves pass through matter almost untouched, so by Earth they were a quiver of 10⁻²¹.

≈ 0.5 c · 75 orbits a second

Half the speed of light

Each black hole was about 200 km across, the size of a small country, and together they held 65 suns. In the final orbits they whirled around each other about 75 times a second at half the speed of light, then touched and merged within a few thousandths of a second.

≈ 250 Hz · fades in 4 ms

The new hole rings like a bell

The merged black hole starts out lopsided and settles into a smooth, spinning shape by shaking off ripples at one pitch. They die away within milliseconds. The pitch and the fade depend only on the hole's mass and spin. GW250114, the loudest event so far, rang clearly enough to confirm Hawking's rule that a black hole's surface can only grow.

35 → 250 Hz · real sound

Why you can hear it

Mergers of star-sized black holes happen to sing in the range of human hearing, so the signal can be played straight through a speaker with no translation. At true pitch GW150914 is a low thump lasting a fraction of a second, below what small speakers can play, which is why this page can move it up two or four octaves.

NGC 4993 · 130 million ly

Neutron-star mergers make gold

When two neutron stars collide, a few percent of a sun is flung out as neutron-rich debris. Its nuclei soak up neutrons faster than they can decay and build heavy elements such as gold, platinum and uranium. The glow of that cloud, a kilonova, was seen after GW170817. It made several Earths' worth of gold.

85 + 66 → 142 suns

The heaviest: GW190521

Stars shouldn't leave black holes between roughly 65 and 130 suns, because a star that big blows itself apart first. GW190521's 85-sun hole sits in that gap, so it was probably born in an earlier merger. The signal lasted about a tenth of a second, only four cycles in the detectors, at a pitch you feel more than hear.

Checking the model

GW150914, simulated and measured

This page doesn't play back a recording. It computes the waveform from the physics, and the numbers on the right come straight out of that calculation for 36 and 29 suns at 410 megaparsecs, next to what LIGO measured.

The long approach uses post-Newtonian theory: Newton's orbits plus Einstein's corrections, worked out to high order, with the orbit shrinking as the waves carry energy away. The final plunge and merger can't be done that way, so the model hands over to a formula fitted to supercomputer simulations of merging black holes. The ringdown uses the true ringing pitch and fade of a spinning black hole, and the final mass and spin come from fits to the same simulations.

The biggest difference is in the timing. LIGO's paper described the signal as rising "over 0.2 s, in about 8 cycles, from 35 to 150 Hz", a rough figure from the simplest version of the theory. With the higher-order terms included, as here, the last stretch goes faster.

How it works: the waveform is the main (quadrupole) gravitational-wave mode of two non-spinning bodies. The inspiral is the TaylorT4 post-Newtonian approximation, with the orbital frequency evolved to 3.5 post-Newtonian order and amplitude to 2nd order. For black holes it hands over at v²/c² = 0.16 to the "backwards one body" merger model of McWilliams (2019), whose frequency rises smoothly to the ringdown tone of the final hole (Berti, Cardoso & Will 2006 fits). Peak amplitude, final mass, final spin and radiated energy follow fits to numerical-relativity simulations. Neutron stars are treated as points until their surfaces, 12 km in radius, touch; after that the page shows a short, faint ringing of the merged star at about 2.8 kHz, the collapse to a black hole, a kilonova and a jet. The kilonova and jet are drawn much faster than their real timescales of seconds to days. Distances convert to redshift with Planck-like cosmology, and every frequency and time is as observed at Earth.

The sheet is a picture, not a measurement. Its dents are shaped like the pull of each body, and its ripples show the wave pattern in the plane of the orbit at the right wavelength, travelling outward at the speed of light and read off at the delayed time for each distance. Their height is enlarged beyond any real scale and grows more gently than the real 1/distance fall-off. The "strain at Earth" readouts assume an average orientation of the orbit and detectors, calibrated so GW150914 peaks near its measured 1.0 × 10⁻²¹. The sound is the computed waveform itself, with only its pitch optionally raised by whole octaves.

Simplifications: spins are ignored except in the final black hole, so events with strongly spinning or precessing black holes (GW190521 among them) come out slightly different from the published values. There is no detector noise; real signals are buried in noise and dug out by matching them against thousands of model waveforms like this one.
