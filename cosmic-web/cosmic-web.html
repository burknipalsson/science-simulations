Field guide

What you are watching

Each note below can set up the simulation to show you. The box is 1 billion light-years (307 megaparsecs) across today. It wraps around at the edges, so matter leaving one face comes back through the opposite one, as if the box were one tile of an endless universe.

380,000 years · 1 part in 100,000

It starts as ripples

The cosmic microwave background shows the early universe was smooth to about 1 part in 100,000. Dark matter had already begun to clump by then, and by the start here (redshift 50) its ripples are several percent. They are too faint to see directly, so the view boosts them and says by how much.

walls · filaments · voids

Threads and empty space

Gravity collapses matter along one direction first, into thin walls. Then walls meet and form filaments. In between, voids empty out and grow until they fill most of the volume. A thin slice shows it best, the way galaxy surveys map the sky in wedges.

10¹⁴–10¹⁵ suns

Clusters sit at the knots

Where several filaments cross, matter keeps flowing in and piles up into galaxy clusters, the heaviest objects held together by gravity. Matter keeps streaming in along the threads. Color by speed to see it falling in at over 1,000 km/s.

84% of all matter

Why dark matter is needed

Ordinary matter was locked to light until 380,000 years and could not clump. After that, its tiny ripples had too little time and too little gravity to grow into galaxies. Dark matter ignores light, so its clumps got a head start. Rerun this universe with the gravity of ordinary matter only: the web barely forms and not a single galaxy lights up.

box grows 51× in size

Expansion against gravity

The usual view divides out the expansion of space so the box keeps one size. In reality it was 51 times smaller at the start. Clumps hold themselves together while space between them stretches. For the last 6 billion years or so, dark energy has sped the expansion up, and the web has nearly stopped growing.

≈ 54 million ly from Virgo

A place like ours

The Milky Way and Andromeda form a small group in a thin wall called the Local Sheet, at the edge of a void and about 54 million light-years from the Virgo Cluster. The simulation can't show us exactly, but it can find a spot with the same surroundings. Our galaxy's dark matter would be a couple of these dots.

10⁵ K in threads · 10⁸ K in clusters

Gas falls in and heats up

Dark matter passes through itself, but gas can't. When gas streams collide on a filament they shock, and their motion turns into heat. Cluster gas reaches 100 million degrees, hot enough to shine in X-rays. Much of the ordinary matter in the universe is this thin, hot gas, hard to see in any telescope.

peak ≈ 10 billion years ago

Galaxies light up at the knots

Where gas collects in small dark-matter halos, it cools and forms stars. Blue points are young stars, fading to gold as they age. The universe made stars fastest about 3.5 billion years after the Big Bang and has slowed by about ten times since. The chart below compares this run with the measured history.

quenched: too hot to cool

Red and dead in the clusters

Inside the heaviest clusters the gas is so hot that it can't cool enough to make new stars. The galaxies there age into red, old-star systems, while galaxies out in the filaments keep forming stars. Astronomers see exactly this split between crowded and quiet parts of the universe.

Deep surveys with Hubble and other telescopes count the stars being made at each cosmic age. The rate climbed for the first 3 billion years, peaked around redshift 2, and has been falling ever since. Galaxies today make stars about ten times more slowly than at that peak.

The gold line is measured live from this run, from how fast its gas turns into stars. It is drawn as the simulation plays, so start from the beginning to see it all. The simulation is tuned to form the right total of stars, but the shape of the curve comes from the physics: halos form, gas falls in, and heating, dark energy and a dwindling supply of fresh gas slow things down.

By the numbers

A billion light-years in a box

Each dot is not a star or a galaxy. A dark matter dot is a parcel weighing about 460 billion suns, roughly the mass of the Milky Way's own dark matter halo. A gas dot carries about 90 billion suns of hydrogen and helium, and as it turns into stars it holds roughly one galaxy's worth. The simulation follows how all of them pull on each other while the space around them expands.

Gravity is worked out the way cosmologists do it in particle-mesh codes. The mass of every dot is spread onto a 3D grid, a Fourier transform solves for the gravitational pull on the whole grid at once, and every dot is pushed along. Gas also feels its own pressure, heats in shocks, and cools by glowing. This page does all of that on your graphics card 400 times between redshift 50 and today.

The starting ripples follow the measured spectrum of density ripples, so the sizes of voids and filaments come out close to those in real galaxy maps. The exact arrangement is random, and each new universe gets a different one.

Gas and galaxies: half the particles are gas (16% of the mass). Their pressure is computed on the same grid as gravity. Gas streams can't pass through each other: each gas particle is steered toward the local mean flow, and the lost motion becomes heat. Converging flows get a von Neumann–Richtmyer shock viscosity, compression heats and expansion cools, and a real cooling curve (hydrogen, helium and some metals) lets dense gas radiate. Reionization heats all gas to 10,000 K by redshift 7.5. Galaxies are far smaller than a grid cell, so star formation uses the extended Press–Schechter theory to estimate how much of each cell's gas sits in halos above 10¹¹ suns. New gas arriving in such halos turns into stars with a 15% efficiency, unless it is above about a million degrees. That efficiency is the one tuned number; it sets the total. The gas physics was checked against a CPU version of the same code.

How it works: a periodic particle-mesh (PM) N-body simulation of cold dark matter in a flat ΛCDM universe (Ωm = 0.31, ΩΛ = 0.69, h = 0.68, σ₈ = 0.81, nₛ = 0.965). Starting positions and velocities come from the Zel'dovich approximation applied to Gaussian ripples with the Eisenstein & Hu (1998) power spectrum. Each step spreads mass onto the grid with cloud-in-cell weights, solves Poisson's equation with a 3D fast Fourier transform, takes 4-point gradients, and advances particles with a leapfrog in the scale factor. All of it runs in WebGL 2 shaders; the physics was checked against a CPU version of the same code.

Simplifications: there is no heating from supernovae or black holes, which is one reason this run has less hot gas than the real universe. Gravity is smoothed over one grid cell (1.2–2.4 million parsecs), so clusters look puffier than they are and single galaxies are too small to form. The "heaviest clump" readout adds up the mass in a 30-million-light-year cube. The ordinary-matter-only run keeps the real expansion history and removes only dark matter's pull.
