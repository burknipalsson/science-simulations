1 · Lift

How a wing really lifts

A wing is a machine for throwing air downward. Tilted a few degrees and curved on top, it bends the passing stream down behind it, and by Newton's third law the air pushes the wing up. The same thing seen as pressure: air sweeping over the curved top speeds up and its pressure drops, air underneath slows and its pressure rises. Both descriptions are true at once; they are two views of one flow.

Watch the stream bend

In the smoke view, follow a streak that passes just above the wing. It rises before it reaches the wing (upwash), races over the top, and leaves the trailing edge heading downhill (downwash). That downward-turned air is the lift.

Below about 10° the lift grows in a straight line with angle, close to the real measurements. Beyond that the air can no longer follow the upper surface: it breaks away into a churning wake and the lift stops growing. That is stall. Our tunnel stalls a few degrees early, at a lift coefficient of about 1.0 instead of 1.6. The real boundary layer on a full-size wing is a fraction of a millimetre thick and turbulent, much thinner than one cell of this simulation, and the model that stands in for it is least accurate exactly where the flow is about to break away.

The popular story that air over the top must "rejoin" the air underneath, and so has to go faster, is false. Send puffs of smoke past the wing and the top puff arrives at the trailing edge well before the bottom one. It never catches up.

2 · Vortex streets

The rhythm of a wake

Put anything blunt in a steady wind and its wake will not stay still. Eddies peel off one side, then the other, in a staggered double row that Theodore von Kármán explained in 1911. The beat is set by one number, the Strouhal number St = f·d / U, which for a cylinder sits close to 0.2 over an enormous range of sizes and speeds. The same pattern forms behind a hair in a breeze and behind an island in the trade winds.

3 · Drag

What it costs to push air aside

Drag grows with the square of speed, and the power to overcome it with the cube: twice as fast costs eight times the power. The drag coefficient packs the effect of shape into one number. A streamlined wing section is around 0.007; a flat plate across the wind is about 1.2. Most of a blunt body's drag is not friction but the low-pressure wake it drags behind it.

4 · Medicine

The sound of a narrowed artery

Blood is a fluid too, and the carotid artery in the neck is a 6 mm pipe carrying it at about a third of a metre per second. That is a Reynolds number of a few hundred: the flow is smooth and silent. Plaque that narrows the artery forces the same blood through a smaller hole, so it must go faster. Downstream the fast jet cannot follow the widening wall. It separates, rolls up into eddies, and breaks into turbulence that drums on the artery wall. Through a stethoscope that is a bruit.

Put the stethoscope on

Probes on the artery wall, a few diameters past the narrowing, record the pressure every time step. The page turns their spectrum into sound at its true pitch, so what you hear has the frequency content the simulation actually produces.

Lees and Dewey (1970) found that a bruit's spectrum rolls off above a break frequency f ≈ U / d, set by the jet's speed U and width d.

The echo rule is Bernoulli's law. Cardiologists estimate the pressure drop across a narrowed valve from the jet speed measured by Doppler ultrasound: ΔP ≈ 4v² (mmHg, with v in m/s). The 4 is just ½ × blood's density (1,060 kg/m³), converted to mmHg. The simulation shows where the rule works and where it does not. At the jet, the drop matches Bernoulli closely (the full form subtracts the speed before the narrowing: 4(v₂² − v₁²)). Further downstream, some of the pressure is recovered as the jet slows. A catheter far downstream reads a smaller drop than the echo predicts, a well-known source of disagreement between the two methods.

Narrowing is measured two ways. Ultrasound reports area, angiography the diameter. A 50% diameter stenosis (NASCET "moderate") blocks 75% of the area; 70% of the diameter (the usual threshold for surgery in a patient with symptoms) blocks 91%.

Field guide

Things to look for

Cylinder · Re 150

Kármán vortex street

Eddies shed alternately from top and bottom, at a Strouhal number near 0.2. They drift downstream in two staggered rows.

Wing · tips on

Wingtip vortices

Air under the wing leaks round the tip to the low pressure on top and twists into two trailing tornadoes. Contrails often curl into them, airports space landings to let them decay, and they are the source of induced drag.

Wing · 20°

Stall

Past the critical angle, the flow separates from the upper surface. Lift stops rising, drag soars, and the wake turns into a turbulent mess the width of the wing.

Tower

Horseshoe vortex

The wind hitting the tower's face is pushed down to the ground and rolls up into a vortex wrapped around the base. It is why plazas at the foot of towers are so gusty.

Ball · Re 300

Hairpin vortices

At this speed the wake of a sphere loses its symmetry and sheds a chain of hairpin-shaped vortex loops, all to one side, the result found by Johnson and Patel (1999).

Car

The wake is the drag

Paint the car with its surface pressure. The nose is red (pushed), the roof and pillars blue (sucked), and the tail sits in low pressure that pulls backwards. Most of a car's drag is this pressure difference, not friction.

Cyclist · tucked vs upright

Hiding in the arms

Going from sitting up to a low tuck cuts the frontal area by about a third. At 40 km/h that saves around 100 watts, more than any lightweight frame can.

Artery · 75%

Jet and recirculation

Beyond the narrowing the jet shoots down the middle while blood near the wall flows backwards in a slow recirculating zone. These low, oscillating wall stresses are where plaque tends to grow.

Ball · Re 100,000

Turbulence

Switch to vortices: the wake fills with a tangle of worm-like vortex tubes, stretched and twisted at every scale, from the size of the ball down to the grid.

How it works

Billions of tiny collisions a second

Instead of solving the equations of fluid flow directly, the lattice Boltzmann method follows clouds of imaginary particles on a 3D grid. Each cell holds 19 numbers: how much fluid is moving in each of 19 directions (standing still, the six faces and the twelve edges of a cube). Every time step has two parts. In stream, each number hops to the neighbouring cell it points at. In collide, the 19 numbers in each cell relax toward the mix expected for gas in local equilibrium. Out of these simple local rules, averaged over many cells, emerge exactly the Navier–Stokes equations, the same equations behind The Atmosphere next door.

Because every cell only talks to its neighbours, the method suits a graphics card perfectly. Here the grid is stored in floating-point textures, and each step is one pass of a fragment shader over all the cells: 3 to 4 billion cell updates per second on an RTX 4090. That is roughly what the fastest supercomputers in the world managed around the year 2000.

Turbulence at real-world Reynolds numbers involves eddies far smaller than a cell. A large-eddy model (Smagorinsky's, from 1963 weather modelling) adds extra viscosity where the resolved flow is strongly sheared, standing in for the eddies the grid cannot see. On the wing, car, cyclist and tower, a wall model stands in for the boundary layer. It is a fraction of a millimetre thick, so the wall uses the law of the wall to slip just enough to feel the right friction. Absorbing layers at the tunnel's edges soak up sound waves, which a lattice gas carries just as real air does. Curved surfaces are placed between grid points by interpolation, so the wing is a smooth aerofoil rather than a staircase.

Lab notes. Two textbook upgrades to the collision step, multiple-relaxation-time and regularized collisions, are usually more stable. Here, both went unstable in the free stream at very low viscosity, as some linear stability analyses predict for moving flows. The simplest operator (BGK) plus absorbing layers won. The first wall model fed back on itself and blew up near the wing's sharp trailing edge, until it learned to stay no-slip at sharp edges, as viscosity does in real life.
