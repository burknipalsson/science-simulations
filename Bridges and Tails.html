Field guide

What you are watching

Each note below can set up the simulation to show you. One time unit here is about 24 million years. Distances are in kiloparsecs: 1 kpc is 3,262 light-years, and the Sun sits about 8 kpc from the center of the Milky Way.

Sun : nearest star ≈ 1 : 29,000,000

The stars almost never touch

Shrink the Sun to a grain of sand and the nearest star is another grain 29 km away. Two galaxies can pass straight through each other and almost no stars collide. Everything you see comes from gravity reshaping their orbits.

Toomre & Toomre, 1972

Tides throw out the tails

The long streams are stars flung out by tides. They form best when a disk spins the same way the other galaxy swings past, because those stars then stay in step with the tidal pull. Run a retrograde flyby and compare the tail count in the readout.

Hα, 656.3 nm

The gas does collide

Gas clouds are huge and fill the disk, so they cannot pass through each other. They crash, compress and collapse into new stars. The pink knots mark where that happens. Hot gas around young stars glows in hydrogen-alpha red, the color that lights up real merger photos.

dynamical friction

Dark matter pulls them together

Each galaxy sits inside a halo of dark matter about ten times heavier than its stars. As the halos overlap they drag on each other and lose orbital energy. The galaxies swing apart, fall back and merge after a few passes. Turn on the center paths to see the orbit shrink.

a bullseye hit

A head-on hit makes a ring

When a small galaxy drops straight through the middle of a disk, the sudden pull inward and the rebound outward send a wave of stars moving out like a ripple in a pond. The Cartwheel galaxy's ring is about 150,000 light-years across.

~1–2 billion years later

Two spirals make an elliptical

After the merger the stars' neat circular orbits are scrambled into random ones. The result is a round, featureless blob, which is what astronomers see in many elliptical galaxies. Most giant ellipticals are thought to be built this way.

Our own collision

The Milky Way and Andromeda

Andromeda is the nearest large galaxy, and it is moving toward us at about 110 km/s. For decades the forecast was a head-on merger in about 4.5 billion years. A 2025 study using newer measurements, including the pull of the Large Magellanic Cloud and the Triangulum galaxy, found the odds are closer to a coin flip within the next 10 billion years.

If it does happen, the Sun will almost certainly survive, because stars don't collide. It may be flung into a long tidal tail or end up far out in the halo of the merged galaxy. Astronomers have already nicknamed that galaxy Milkomeda.

How it works: each galaxy is a heavy dark-matter halo (a softened Plummer sphere) with a disk and bulge of massless stars and gas clouds that feel both halos. This is the restricted N-body method Alar and Juri Toomre used in "Galactic Bridges and Tails" (1972). The two halos pull on each other and feel a simple drag while they overlap, which stands in for dynamical friction. Units: 1 length unit = 8 kpc, 1 mass unit = 2 × 10¹¹ solar masses, so 1 time unit ≈ 24 million years.

Simplifications: stars don't pull on each other, halos stay rigid, and the gas has no pressure. The pink starburst glow is a rule of thumb: it lights up gas from the two galaxies meeting in the same small region at over 100 km/s. Real mergers need full hydrodynamic simulations on supercomputers.
