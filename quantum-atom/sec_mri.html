A medical aside · magnetic resonance imaging

Listening to the body's protons

Every hydrogen nucleus is a single proton, and a proton has a quantum property called spin that makes it a tiny magnet. An MRI scanner images these protons, nearly all of them in the body's water (the molecules of The Hydrogen Bond) and fat. It never sees the electrons at all. Three numbers do the work: how fast the protons precess in the field, and two relaxation times, T1 and T2, that differ from tissue to tissue.

What the scanner's coil picks up

At 3 tesla, out of every million protons only about ten more line up with the field than against it.

That tiny excess, about 10 per million at body temperature, is all the scanner has to work with. It is enough because a single cubic millimetre of water holds about 67 billion billion hydrogen nuclei. Each spin precesses around the field at the Larmor frequency, 42.58 MHz per tesla: 127.7 MHz in a 3 T scanner, just above the FM radio band. A radio pulse at exactly that frequency tips the spins over; as they precess together they broadcast back, and the coil hears them.

The art is in the timing. T2 is how long the spins keep in step after the pulse; T1 is how long they take to realign with the field. Fluid has long T1 and T2, fat short T1. A short repeat time and echo time (T1-weighting) makes fat bright and fluid dark, good for anatomy. A long repeat and echo (T2-weighting) makes water bright, and most disease, from oedema around a tumour to inflammation or a stroke some hours old, carries extra water. Gadolinium contrast agents shorten T1 wherever they leak out of damaged blood vessels, so those spots light up on a T1-weighted scan.
