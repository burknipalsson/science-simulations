Quantum tunnelling

Walking through walls

Here the electron runs along a line at a wall it does not have the energy to climb. A thrown ball would bounce off every time. The electron's wave does not stop dead at the wall: it dies away inside, exponentially. If the wall is thin enough, a little of it is still alive on the far side, and that is the chance the electron turns up there. Thicken the wall by a fraction of a nanometre and that chance collapses.

Chance of getting through

against wall thickness, logarithmic scale · line: exact calculation · dots: each run of the simulation above

The curve is a straight line on a log scale: every extra slice of wall divides the chance by the same factor.

With the settings above, an electron 3 eV short of the top of the wall, each 0.1 nm of extra thickness cuts the odds about six-fold; for an electron leaving a metal, about 4.5 eV short, it is ten-fold. That steep, exact dependence is not a curiosity. A great deal of nature and technology runs on it.

A tenth of a nanometre · the hydrogen atom

Where the electron lives

Put the electron wave in a box of its own making, the pull of a single proton, and it can only settle into certain shapes, each with an exact energy. These are the orbitals chemistry is built from. They are not paths; they are standing waves, and the brightness shows where the electron is likely to be found. Everything here comes straight from the solution of Schrödinger's equation for hydrogen, drawn in three dimensions on your graphics card.

The simplest chemical bond · H₂⁺

One electron, two protons

Bring a second proton near a hydrogen atom and the electron's wave spreads over both. If it settles in the bonding shape, it piles up between the protons and its pull on each of them outweighs their repulsion: the two are held together. This is the hydrogen molecular ion, the one molecule whose Schrödinger equation can be solved exactly, and every chemical bond is a more crowded version of it.

A medical aside · magnetic resonance imaging

Listening to the body's protons

Every hydrogen nucleus is a single proton, and a proton has a quantum property called spin that makes it a tiny magnet. An MRI scanner images these protons, nearly all of them in the body's water (the molecules of The Hydrogen Bond) and fat. It never sees the electrons at all. Three numbers do the work: how fast the protons precess in the field, and two relaxation times, T1 and T2, that differ from tissue to tissue.

What the scanner's coil picks up

signal (gold: spins in step across the field, which induce the signal) and recovery along the field (green), against time in milliseconds

At 3 tesla, out of every million protons only about ten more line up with the field than against it.

That tiny excess, about 10 per million at body temperature, is all the scanner has to work with. It is enough because a single cubic millimetre of water holds about 67 billion billion hydrogen nuclei. Each spin precesses around the field at the Larmor frequency, 42.58 MHz per tesla: 127.7 MHz in a 3 T scanner, just above the FM radio band. A radio pulse at exactly that frequency tips the spins over; as they precess together they broadcast back, and the coil hears them.

The art is in the timing. T2 is how long the spins keep in step after the pulse; T1 is how long they take to realign with the field. Fluid has long T1 and T2, fat short T1. A short repeat time and echo time (T1-weighting) makes fat bright and fluid dark, good for anatomy. A long repeat and echo (T2-weighting) makes water bright, and most disease, from oedema around a tumour to inflammation or a stroke some hours old, carries extra water. Gadolinium contrast agents shorten T1 wherever they leak out of damaged blood vessels, so those spots light up on a T1-weighted scan.

Honest numbers

How close is this to nature?

Every simulation on this page was checked against an exact result or a measurement before any graphics were drawn, using the same code that runs here. The quantum world is a good place for this, because its simplest cases can be solved exactly.

The electron waves (the main stage and the tunnelling lab) use the split-operator method: the wave is advanced alternately in position space, where the walls act, and in momentum space, where its motion is exact, with a fast Fourier transform carrying it between the two. It conserves probability by construction. On the graphics card one step, two Fourier transforms of a million-point grid, takes about a third of a millisecond, and the result agrees with an independent double-precision version on the CPU.

The hydrogen atom is drawn from its exact solution: Laguerre polynomials for the distance from the proton, spherical harmonics for the direction. The decay rates come from the exact overlap of each pair of orbitals. H₂⁺ is solved exactly too, in coordinates shaped like rugby balls around the two protons, where its equation separates into two simple ones. MRI uses the Bloch equations, the standard description of how proton spins tip, dephase and recover.

What is left out: the main stage is two-dimensional, the electron's spin and relativity are ignored (they shift hydrogen's lines by about one part in 70,000), the protons are held still while the electron moves, and the which-slit detector is an ideal, instantaneous measurement.
