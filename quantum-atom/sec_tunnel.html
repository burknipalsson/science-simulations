Quantum tunnelling

Walking through walls

Here the electron runs along a line at a wall it does not have the energy to climb. A thrown ball would bounce off every time. The electron's wave does not stop dead at the wall: it dies away inside, exponentially. If the wall is thin enough, a little of it is still alive on the far side, and that is the chance the electron turns up there. Thicken the wall by a fraction of a nanometre and that chance collapses.

The curve is a straight line on a log scale: every extra slice of wall divides the chance by the same factor.

With the settings above, an electron 3 eV short of the top of the wall, each 0.1 nm of extra thickness cuts the odds about six-fold; for an electron leaving a metal, about 4.5 eV short, it is ten-fold. That steep, exact dependence is not a curiosity. A great deal of nature and technology runs on it.
