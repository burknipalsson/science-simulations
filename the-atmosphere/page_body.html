The engine of the winds

Slice the atmosphere from pole to pole

Average the model's air all the way around each circle of latitude and you see the machinery that drives the weather. Warm air rises near the equator, flows toward the poles high up, sinks around 30° and returns along the ground as the trade winds. This loop is the Hadley cell, the white contours below. Earth's spin turns the poleward-moving air eastward, and that builds the jet streams, the orange cores. Everything here is averaged over the last couple of weeks of simulated weather and keeps updating.

Other worlds

Change the planet and watch the weather reorganise

The same physics runs any planet you give it. Speed up the spin and the jet stream splits into bands like Jupiter's. Slow it down and the tropics spread to the poles. Lock one face to the sun and a permanent storm forms beneath it. Use the controls under the globe, then come back here to see the new climate in the slice above.

Checking the model

How close is it to the real Earth?

The model is simplified. Its air is 15 layers deep, and its columns are about 300 km apart, too coarse to resolve a single thunderstorm. It has no real clouds in its sunlight, only an average reflectivity. Its ocean is a 50-metre slab that stores heat, with the transport of the real currents added as a fixed pattern.

Even so, the big features come out close to the measured ones. The values on the left update live from the running model, averaged over recent simulated weeks. The real values are long-term averages from satellites and weather balloons for the same time of year where that matters.

Before any of the weather was added, the core that moves the air passed two standard tests used for professional models. In the Held–Suarez test it made jet streams of 30 m/s near 45° and surface westerlies of 8–10 m/s, as the reference models do. In the Jablonowski–Williamson test an initially smooth jet broke into a storm that deepened to 941 hPa after 9 days, close to the published reference solutions, while total energy was conserved to one part in 100 million.

How it works

A weather model in a web page

This is a spectral primitive-equation model, the same basic design that national weather services ran from the 1980s until recently. The winds, temperature, surface pressure and moisture of the whole atmosphere are stored as sums of 946 spherical harmonics, smooth wave patterns that fit a sphere perfectly and have no trouble at the poles. At every step each field is turned into values on a grid of 128 × 64 points and 15 levels, the physics is computed column by column, and the result is turned back into waves.

The equations are Newton's laws for moving air on a rotating planet, the first law of thermodynamics, and the conservation of mass and water. Heat comes from sunlight that follows the real Sun through the day and the seasons. The air loses heat as infrared radiation, and water carries enormous amounts of energy as it evaporates from the sea and condenses in clouds.

Your computer splits the planet into latitude bands and runs them on several processor cores at once. The graphics card draws the result: real NASA imagery of the ground, snow and sea ice from the model, and cloud texture carried along by the model's own winds.
